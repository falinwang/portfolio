Firm-Level Review Management

A two-platform workflow problem solved by a single reframe: the table isn't a data display. It's a triage tool.

The Decision That Mattered Most

The table isn't a data display. It's a triage tool.

That reframe didn't come at kickoff. It came in week 7, after the CEO demo.

The original brief asked for a "firm-level review dashboard" — a way to show all of a firm's review data in one place. The first six weeks of design treated it that way: I built a flat table that surfaced every column the PM, AM team, and Engineering had requested. It tested fine in internal critique. It looked complete.

Then the CEO saw it and called it unclear and overwhelming.

That feedback could have been answered cosmetically — tighten the spacing, soften the borders, ship it. Instead I took it as a signal that the underlying model was wrong. Firm admins don't want to read a table. They want to know who needs my attention right now.

Once that became the design's job, every subsequent decision — the three-tier hierarchy, the icon-plus-color status system, the conditional search, the reframed zero state — fell out of that one principle.

The IA restructure took ~3 weeks in the middle of an already-running project, with no scope change and no extension. That trade-off — slower middle, cleaner outcome — was the project's most consequential decision.

The Problem

Firm admins had to use two platforms to do one job

Martindale-Avvo sells reputation management to law firms. The promise is simple — help attorneys get more reviews, respond faster, look credible online. But the tool meant to deliver that promise was broken at the seam.

Firm administrators — the people responsible for an entire firm's online presence — had to use two separate platforms to do one job:

MyMA

Attorney profiles and marketing — where firm admins managed profiles and visibility.

CSC (legacy)

Review management — where actual review data lived, completely separate from profiles.

Every time they needed to check review status, respond to a client, or identify which attorney needed attention, they had to leave one platform and log into another. The workflow was never complete in either place.

The business was expanding from attorney-level to firm-level accounts. Firms with 10, 50, even 100+ attorneys were becoming key revenue. Without a unified tool, Account Managers couldn't scale support — and firm admins couldn't self-serve.

Users

Three audiences. One design had to serve all of them.

Primary

Firm Administrator

Responsible for the firm's entire online reputation. May be a marketing lead, an operations manager, or a paralegal wearing the marketing hat. They don't have time to read a table — they need to know who needs attention right now.

Secondary

Account Manager

Internal team supporting larger firm accounts. Without firm-level tooling, AMs were doing manual triage work that didn't scale past ~10-attorney firms.

Edge Case

Single-Attorney "Firm"

Solo practitioners technically registered as firms. The design had to gracefully degrade — no point showing a 100-row hierarchy when there's one attorney.

Research & Discovery

No formal study. Three structured sources.

Source 1 — Existing System Audit

Walked through the current MyMA attorney-level review page and CSC peer review management side by side. Mapped every point where the user journey broke or required platform switching.

Source 2 — Account Manager Interviews

Ran a dedicated AM review session before development. Key signal: 100+ attorney firms had no way to identify who needed attention. There was no urgency signal — no way to see at a glance which attorney hadn't received a review in over a year.

Source 3 — Stakeholder Feedback Loop

Used weekly design reviews with PM, Product, and Engineering as continuous discovery — surfacing edge cases (single-attorney firms, zero-review firms, multi-location firms) that shaped the final IA.

Three insights that changed the design

1. The table wasn't a data display problem — it was a triage problem.

Firm admins don't want to read a table. They want to know who needs them. This reframed the design from "show all data" to "surface urgency."

2. The hierarchy was invisible when flattened.

Firm + All Attorneys + Individual Attorneys sounds simple. But flattened into a table, users couldn't tell what was a summary row vs. an actionable row. The IA needed to be felt, not just labeled.

3. Zero reviews is a different product moment.

A firm with no reviews isn't an empty state — it's a user who doesn't yet believe reviews matter. The design needed to sell the value before showing the tool.

Value, Not Features

What the design needed to deliver — in order

Before designing anything, I listed what the design had to accomplish for the user — not what features the PM had listed. Anything that didn't serve one of these got cut.

1
Show me who needs attention — urgency before completeness
2
Show me the firm's overall health — not just individual attorneys
3
Let me act without leaving the page — close the cross-platform gap
4
Don't make me learn a new mental model — respect the existing system
5
Motivate me if I have nothing yet — zero state as a product moment, not an empty state

Four Key Design Decisions

Each one came with a wrong answer first

01 · Information Hierarchy

How to show the hierarchy

A.
Flat table. All rows equal weight. Simple to build. Users couldn't distinguish summary rows from attorney rows. Leadership called it "unclear and overwhelming."
Failed
B.
Separate cards above table. Pull summary data out, show as cards on top. Creates two mental models — users have to reconcile card data with table data.
Failed
C. ✓
Three-tier nested table with visual separation. Single mental model. Hierarchy is felt through visual weight, not just labels. Summary rows stay locked during search/sort so the frame of reference never moves.
Shipped
Manage All Reviews        ← sticky CTA row, distinct background
├── Firm Reviews            ← section header, firm-only data
└── All Attorney Reviews    ← rollup section header
    ├── Attorney A            ← indented, sortable
    ├── Attorney B
    └── Attorney C

"The table isn't showing data — it's showing a business. The hierarchy has to match how the admin thinks about their firm, not how the database is structured."

[ V1 flat table → V2 three-tier hierarchy comparison ]
02 · Status Indicators

How to signal review health at a glance

A.
Color only. Green/yellow/red per row. Fails WCAG 2.2 — color-blind users get no signal. Approximately 8% of male users affected.
Failed
B.
Text labels only. "On Track" / "Warning" / "Urgent." Too verbose for a dense table. Kills scannability — the one thing this system had to do.
Failed
C. ✓
Icon + color dual system. Accessible, scannable, self-explanatory. Passes WCAG 2.2 without adding visual noise. The icon does the triage work in under a second.
Shipped
✓ On track (reviewed within 90 days) ⚠ Caution (3 months – 1 year) 🚨 Urgent (over 1 year or zero reviews)

Each status includes a tooltip explaining the threshold on hover. WCAG 2.2 compliant — icon carries the signal, color reinforces it.

[ Status indicator system — before (color only) / after (icon + color + tooltip) ]
03 · Zero State

What to show when a firm has no reviews

A.
Empty table with message. "No reviews yet." No motivation to act. High drop-off risk — user has no reason to return.
Failed
B.
Full onboarding flow. Multi-step education before the dashboard. Too much friction. Users who already have reviews get blocked unnecessarily.
Failed
C. ✓
Two-layer contextual zero state. Respects users who already have reviews while actively motivating those who don't. The banner creates an ongoing nudge without blocking the UI.
Shipped

Layer 1 — Entry Popup

Triggers only for zero-review firms. Explains why reviews matter + single CTA to start requesting. Clears after first interaction.

Layer 2 — Persistent Banner

"88% of consumers consider an attorney's reputation before hiring." Data-backed value message visible to all users — gives returning users a reason to act.

[ Zero state — entry popup + persistent banner ]
04 · Conditional UI for Scale

When to show what — two-attorney firms and 100-attorney firms can't use the same UI

Small firms shouldn't pay the cognitive cost of features built for large firms. Large firms can't function without them. The solution: conditional rendering based on attorney count.

  • → Search bar: appears only when attorney count > 10
  • → Pagination: activates at > 10 items
  • → Status urgency filter: auto-prioritized to top when filter is "Needs Attention"
  • → Filter pattern: all options visible by default, uncheck to exclude (Google Flights model)
  • → Summary rows: locked — not affected by search or sort

"A search bar that only appears when you need it is more trustworthy than one that's always visible. It signals: the product knows what kind of firm you are."

[ Small firm (search hidden) vs. large firm (search visible + filter active) ]
Scope Discipline

Naming what wasn't designed was as important as what shipped

It's how the project stayed on scope across 13 weeks, a mid-project CEO demo, and 20+ design reviews.

In Scope — Phase 1
✓Review summary dashboard for firm admins
✓Multi-source data consolidation (Avvo, Lawyers.com, Martindale-Hubbell)
✓Attorney-level health monitoring within firm view
✓Badge/award display and download
✓Zero-state onboarding for firms with no reviews
Explicitly Out of Scope
✕Peer review management — stays in CSC. Different audience, different workflow. Merging it now would have doubled complexity for a 5% use case.
✕Native review generation — future phase. Worth building right rather than rushing.
✕FindLaw integration — pending separate data architecture work.
Final Design

Four surfaces. One mental model.

Review Summary Dashboard

Three-tier nested table with locked summary rows. Color + icon status system. Real-time attorney name search (conditional on count > 10). Sortable columns: review count, star rating, last review date. Hover tooltip showing platform breakdown across Avvo, Lawyers.com, and Martindale-Hubbell.

Badge & Award Display

Horizontal tier progression: Silver → Gold → Platinum. Earned badges shown in full color with copy-code and download options. Unearned badges grayscale, info only. Year selector for annual Avvo awards.

Zero State

Entry popup with value proposition and single CTA. Persistent banner with social proof statistic. Red-line visualization showing zero across all metrics — making the absence feel like a problem to solve, not a UI bug.

Review Management Page

Filtered view based on summary page entry point. Platform breakdown across Avvo, Lawyers.com, Martindale-Hubbell, and Peer Reviews. Firm vs. attorney review differentiation. Respond, highlight, and request-review actions inline.

If Not Me

What doesn't get built without these decisions

The consequences of the default path — flat table, color-only status, empty zero state — aren't visible on launch day. They appear as silent abandonment over weeks.

Invisible urgency

Firms with 100+ attorneys continue to have no signal for which attorney is at risk. Admins can't prioritize. Review health deteriorates without awareness — and the AM team is still doing manual triage.

Accessibility liability

Color-only status signals fail 8% of male users with color-blindness. A system that looks complete but fails a WCAG 2.2 audit isn't a finished product — it's a compliance risk.

Zero-state drop-off

Without the entry popup and persistent banner, zero-review firms open an empty table, don't know what to do, and close the tab. The feature exists — but the behavior never starts.

Reflection

最難的部分不是視覺設計

最難的部分不是視覺設計 — 是忍住什麼都想塞進去的衝動。

Every stakeholder had a different definition of what "firm-level" meant. The version that shipped wasn't the most feature-rich one. It was the version where every element had a clear job — and nothing was there just because someone asked for it.

The reframe — the table isn't a data display, it's a triage tool — wasn't a clever insight. It was a forcing function. It gave me a way to say no without saying no to people; I could say no to elements that didn't serve triage.

"The hardest design work is often deciding what the design is for, not what it looks like."
