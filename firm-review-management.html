Firm-Level Review Management

Solving a two-platform workflow problem by designing a three-tier information architecture — from fragmented legacy systems to a unified reputation dashboard for law firms.

[ Final dashboard — firm + attorney review table ]
Role

Lead Product Designer — end-to-end, kickoff to dev handoff

Collaborators

PM · Senior Director of Product · Engineering · Account Management · CEO

Timeline

~8 weeks · 20+ design review sessions

Outcome

Zero major revision requests post-handoff · AM team validated pre-dev · CEO presentation incorporated without scope change

The Problem

Firm admins had to use two platforms to do one job

Law firms managing multiple attorneys had no single place to oversee their firm's online reputation. They were forced to jump between two separate platforms — MyMA for attorney profiles and the legacy CSC system for review management — to monitor reviews, respond to clients, and track attorney performance.

Every task required context-switching. Every workflow was incomplete. Firms weren't managing their reviews — not because they didn't care, but because the tools made it too hard.

"The broken user journey between MyMA and the legacy CSC system was creating invisible churn — firms weren't managing their reviews because the tools made it too hard."

The ask: integrate firm-level review management into MyMA.
The real problem: a fragmented information architecture that no single dashboard had ever solved.

[ Before state — two-platform workflow: MyMA + CSC ]
The Challenge

Two problems in one

1. Broken user journey

Firm admins needed MyMA for attorney profiles and CSC for review management. No single view. No unified workflow. Every task started in the wrong place.

2. Complex information hierarchy

A law firm isn't flat. It has a firm entity with its own reviews, multiple attorneys each with reviews across 3 platforms, and aggregate views that combine both. Showing this in a single table without overwhelming users was the core design challenge.

Discovery

What the data and AM interviews revealed

Starting from the existing attorney-level review management page, I mapped data coverage across Avvo, Lawyers.com, and Martindale-Hubbell, traced where the current workflow broke down for firm admins, and ran a review session with the Account Management team to understand what they needed to support their clients.

Key insight from AM session: firms with 100+ attorneys had no way to quickly identify who needed attention. There was no urgency signal — no way to see at a glance which attorney hadn't received a review in over a year.

That missing urgency signal became the design brief: the dashboard needed to function not as a data display, but as a health monitoring tool.

The Decision That Mattered Most

Rejecting the flat table after leadership review

The first version of the dashboard showed everything in a single flat table — firm reviews and attorney reviews side by side, undifferentiated. Leadership feedback after the first stakeholder review was immediate:

"Unclear and overwhelming."

The fix wasn't decoration — it was architecture. The table needed to reflect how firms actually think about their reputation: the firm as a whole, then the attorneys as individual contributors.

Manage All Reviews          ← top-level CTA
├── Firm Reviews             ← firm-only data
└── All Attorney Reviews     ← rollup
    ├── Attorney A
    ├── Attorney B
    └── Attorney C

Iterated through indentation, background color, section dividers, and tooltips before landing on a solution that made the hierarchy scannable without adding visual noise.

Four Design Decisions

The details that made it trustworthy

01 · Information Hierarchy

Three tiers: firm / rollup / individual

Solving for the firm vs. attorney distinction required a clear visual system for each level. The hierarchy needed to be scannable for a firm admin managing 100+ attorneys — and immediately readable for someone checking in on a single attorney's review health.

"The table isn't showing data — it's showing a business. The hierarchy has to match how the admin thinks about their firm, not how the database is structured."

02 · Status Indicators — ADA Compliance

Color alone fails. Icon + color is the standard.

Early designs used color alone to signal review health. The problem: color-only systems fail WCAG 2.2 for color-blind users, and they fail non-color-blind users who are scanning quickly in low-attention states.

✓ On track (reviewed within 90 days) ⚠ Caution (3 months – 1 year) 🚨 Urgent (over 1 year or zero reviews)

Each status includes a tooltip explaining the threshold on hover. WCAG 2.2 compliant — icon carries the signal, color reinforces it.

[ Status indicator system — before (color only) / after (icon + color + tooltip) ]
03 · Zero State Design

Two-layer entry for accounts with no reviews

A significant portion of firm accounts had zero reviews. An empty table with no context is a dead end — users don't know what they're looking at or what to do next. The zero state had to convert confusion into action.

Layer 1 — Entry modal

Pop-up on first visit explains why reviews matter + immediate CTA to start requesting. Clears after first interaction.

Layer 2 — Persistent banner

"88% of consumers consider an attorney's reputation before hiring." Data-backed value message always visible — gives returning users a reason to act.

[ Zero state — entry modal + persistent banner ]
04 · Search & Filter for Scale

Conditional search that appears when you need it

Firms with 100+ attorneys needed to find specific people fast. A persistent search bar on a 5-attorney firm is visual noise. The solution: conditional rendering based on firm size.

  • → Search appears only when attorney count exceeds 10
  • → Real-time name filter — no page reload
  • → Top 3 summary rows locked — not affected by filter
  • → Filter pattern: all options visible by default, uncheck to exclude (Google Flights model)

"A search bar that only appears when you need it is more trustworthy than one that's always visible. It signals: the product knows what kind of firm you are."

[ Search + filter — small firm (no search bar) / large firm (search active) ]
If Not Me

What doesn't get built without these decisions

The consequences of the default path — flat table, color-only status, empty zero state — aren't visible on launch day. They appear as silent abandonment over weeks.

Invisible urgency

Firms with 100+ attorneys continue to have no signal for which attorney is at risk. Admins can't prioritize. Review health deteriorates without awareness.

Accessibility failure

Color-only status signals fail 8% of male users with color-blindness. A system that looks complete but fails a compliance audit is a liability, not a feature.

Zero-state drop-off

Without the entry modal and persistent banner, zero-review firms open an empty table, don't know what to do, and close the tab. The feature exists — but the behavior never starts.

What I Learned

The biggest unlock wasn't a design pattern

Designing for complex B2B hierarchies isn't just an information architecture problem — it's a trust problem. Every label, every tooltip, every empty state is a moment where the product either earns or loses the user's confidence that the system understands their business.

The biggest unlock wasn't a design pattern. It was reframing the table from a data display into a health monitoring tool — giving firm admins a reason to come back every week, not just when a review appeared.

"When I stopped asking 'how do we show all the reviews?' and started asking 'what does a firm admin need to know to act?' — the hierarchy resolved itself."
