AI-Powered Legal Intake

Integrating AI into a high-anxiety, deadline-constrained legal contact form — designing for trust, error recovery, and pivot decisions.

Lead Product Designer — end-to-end

Major legal marketplace · Consumer-facing AI features · Q1 Bundle

Feb 1 hard deadline · AI dependency uncertainty · Scope pivot mid-project

What I designed

AI loading states · Error recovery · Two-step flow · Layout architecture · 6 micro-decisions

Outcome

Shipped to Q1 deadline · A/B test live against standard form · Expanding to paid profiles

Thesis

"Design for trust — make AI feel helpful, not technical."

The Problem

Users don't know what to say. Attorneys don't know what they're getting.

When users contact an attorney on the platform, they're typically in a high-anxiety state — facing a legal problem they don't fully understand. The result: messages with insufficient information, making it hard for attorneys to prioritize or respond effectively. Message quality was notoriously bad, but there was no quality metric to prove it — only submission volume.

User problem

Blank form + legal anxiety = low-quality messages. Users don't know what information matters.

Business problem

Poor message quality reduces attorney engagement. No quality baseline metric existed to measure against.

The Constraints

Three pressures hitting at once

This project didn't have the luxury of ideal conditions. The design had to succeed within four hard constraints — and the most important design decisions came directly from accepting them, not fighting them.

The Decision That Mattered Most

The pivot: choosing Option 2 over Option 3

When the competitor messaging template issue surfaced mid-project, the team faced a fork. Three options were on the table — and choosing the wrong one would either miss the deadline or deliver an incomplete experience.

Option 1 — Parallel templates

Build a new flow, maintain the existing competitor messaging template and the new flow in parallel.

❌ Rejected — engineering cost too high, creates long-term maintenance debt

✅ Option 2 — MVP scope reduction (chosen)

Reduce V1 scope. Add AI questions on top of the existing form only — minimal surface change, maximum feasibility.

Chosen because: existing template is "not the worst on site." A functional AI feature on a working form beats a beautiful but unshippable redesign.

Option 3 — Full redesign including competitor messaging

Redesign everything — contact form, competitor messaging, and new AI layer simultaneously.

❌ Rejected — technically blocked, deadline-impossible

"The question wasn't which option was most elegant — it was which option would actually ship with value intact. Constraint-driven scope cuts are design decisions too."

Chapter 2 — Flow Architecture

The two-step structure: reduce cognitive load first, qualify second

The original form asked for everything at once. The redesigned flow splits the experience into two moments with different purposes.

Step 1 — Message

User describes their legal problem (100-character minimum). Focus: get the user writing. Nothing else on screen.

→ Clicks Next → AI begins analysis

Step 2 — AI + Send

AI returns up to 2 follow-up questions → user adds detail → communication preferences → submit.

Communication preferences moved here to reduce initial form length

Chapter 3 — Micro-Decisions

Six decisions. Each one a deliberate trade-off.

The details of an AI feature are where trust is won or lost. These six decisions — some made quickly, some challenged and revised — collectively define how the AI feels to the user.

Decision 1

Remove the AI icon

An AI icon in the loading state would signal "this is AI-powered" to the consumer. But the product's AI branding strategy was deliberately attorney-facing — AI labels appeared on attorney email insights and other B2B features, not on consumer surfaces. Putting an AI icon here would create a brand inconsistency.

Decision: Text-only loading state — "Analyzing your case details"

"The loading message is the experience. It tells the user the system is working for them — without making them feel like they're being processed by a machine."

[ Loading state — before / after ]
Decision 2

Remove the "optional" label from AI questions

Labeling AI-generated follow-up questions as "optional" gives users permission to skip them — but these questions are the mechanism that improves message quality. If users skip them, the whole value proposition breaks.

Decision: Remove optional label. Conversion rate over psychological escape hatch.

Risk acknowledged: tested via A/B to validate no negative impact on drop-off rate.

Decision 3

Silent failure when AI generates nothing

If the AI fails to generate questions — whether due to a technical error, non-legal content, or an edge case — showing an error state punishes the user for a system failure they didn't cause. The form should still complete successfully.

Decision: Silent skip to communication preferences. No error message. No broken experience.

"Users should only have to take responsibility for their own decisions — not for system failures they didn't cause and can't fix."

Decision 4 — Challenged by PM

Modal warning on edit: proposed, then revised

I proposed a modal warning: if the user tried to edit their original message after AI had generated questions, a warning would appear explaining that doing so would clear and regenerate the AI questions. My reasoning: users would lose work without knowing it.

The PM pushed back: AI questions are optional anyway. If the user edits their message, they want better questions — that's intentional. The reset cost is low. The modal is friction that doesn't justify its cost.

Decision: Modal added (not removed) — but the pushback sharpened the framing.

"This challenge made me articulate the difference between friction that protects and friction that just obstructs. The modal stays — but now I can explain exactly why."

Decision 5

Input box: 3 rows default

Single-line input feels too constrained for a message that needs to describe a legal situation. Competitor analysis showed a range of default heights. A 3-row input signals "write a paragraph, not a sentence" without being intimidating.

Decision: 3 rows — grounded in competitive benchmarking, not gut feel.

Decision 6 — The Layout Flip

Attorney card left, form right, CTA inline

The original layout placed the attorney info panel on the right and the form on the left — with the submit button anchored to the bottom of the attorney card. A user who finished filling out the form had to cross to the right side of the screen to submit. The button was spatially disconnected from the action.

A prior workshop identified this as a disconnected button issue. The solution: flip the layout — attorney card left, form right, CTA inline below the form. On mobile, CTA sits directly beneath the input fields.

Decision: Layout flip + CTA inline, consistent with Q&A and review forms.

"Layout is not an aesthetic decision — it's an affordance decision. The user's mental model is 'fill in → press the button right below.' The original layout broke that model."

If Not Me

What the default path looked like

Each decision had a lower-effort default. Here's what ships without the deliberate choices:

Spinning loader

No text context. Users see an indeterminate spinner and assume the page is broken. Drop rate increases.

Error state on AI failure

Users see "Something went wrong" for a problem they didn't cause. Damages trust at the worst possible moment.

Disconnected submit button

Users fill out the form and look for a button where the form ends. It's on the other side of the screen. Hesitation, confusion, abandonment.

What I'd Do Differently

The decisions I'd make earlier next time

Define message quality before designing

We were optimizing for message quality without a baseline metric. I'd push to define — even roughly — what "good" looks like before designing the intervention. Without a baseline, we can only measure relative change, not absolute improvement.

Include competitor messaging in scope earlier

The scope pivot to Option 2 was the right call given the constraints, but the constraint itself could have been anticipated. A fuller discovery phase would have surfaced the competitor messaging dependency before it became a mid-project blocker.

Map the AOP answer overlap earlier

The risk that AI would re-ask questions already answered in the AOP was discovered mid-design, not at discovery. I'd audit all existing data inputs before designing an AI layer — to understand what the model already knows about the user.

Plan the post-MVP roadmap alongside MVP

The full redesign (Option 3) still needs to happen. By shipping Option 2 first, we've created a temporary state that will require follow-up work. I'd document the post-MVP roadmap explicitly so it's easier to pick up — and harder to deprioritize.
