Project Overview

The Situation

A high-traffic page in sustained decline

A legal tech company's highest-traffic attorney review discovery page was experiencing a sustained organic traffic decline. Stakeholders described it as a page that needed refreshing. The design looked dated, and the numbers weren't moving.

I was brought in as the sole designer to lead the redesign — responsible for reframing the problem, designing the end-to-end filter system and review card experience, and aligning a cross-functional team around a unified direction.

My Scope
  • Problem reframing
  • Content hierarchy restructure
  • Geo + Practice Area filter system
  • Review card redesign
  • Stakeholder alignment across 5 functions

The Decision That Mattered Most

"The hardest part wasn't designing the page. It was deciding what kind of problem this actually was."

The brief described this as a page that needed refreshing. Traffic was declining, the design looked dated, and stakeholders wanted it improved.

The real problem was different: the page was architecturally misaligned with its own users.

Users search "lawyer reviews" and land on a grid of practice area directory links. That's not a visual problem — it's a content hierarchy problem. Every design decision that followed came from that reframing.

If that judgment had been wrong — if we'd treated this as a visual refresh — we would have rearranged deck chairs on a page that would have kept losing ground.

What Changed — And Why It Mattered Beyond the Design

1

Content hierarchy restructure

Reviews moved above the fold. Practice area directory links moved to the bottom.

This decision drove the PM to reframe the PRD entirely — from "page improvement" to "Trust Engine." The design didn't follow the PRD. The PRD followed the design direction.

[VISUAL: Before/after page structure diagram — section order changing. Labels only.]
2

Geo + Practice Area filter system

Two independent filters — location and practice area — with free-form auto-suggest.

The original ticket specified a metro area dropdown. I changed this to free-form + auto-suggest after evaluating three approaches. A dropdown locks users into our taxonomy. Auto-suggest meets users at the word they're already thinking.

This also forced a conversation about backend data completeness that hadn't been raised before — directly shaping the PM's scope decision.

[VISUAL: Filter component — default, typing (auto-suggest dropdown), and selected (chip applied) states. Desktop.]
3

Review card redesign

Added three fields: City/State, Primary Practice Area, recency timestamp.

Not for aesthetics — each field answers one question a mid-funnel user needs in under 5 seconds: Is this attorney near me? Do they handle my case? Is this recent?

Without these signals, every card looks identical. Users scan without engaging.

[VISUAL: Review card before vs. after. Old: name, rating, snippet. New: photo, name, rating, City/State, AOP, timestamp, "See Full Review."]

A Decision I Reversed — And Why That's Part of the Story

I confirmed the search bar would be removed. The rationale was sound: primary users are in browsing mode, not search mode.

In the final stakeholder walkthrough, two PMs raised a valid concern: secondary users — people who want to leave a review — need a way to find their attorney first. The search bar served that flow.

I relinquished the decision and redesigned the search bar as a secondary, lower-priority path.

What made this work: every decision had been documented with rationale throughout the project. When the reversal happened, it wasn't chaos — it was a controlled scope adjustment with a clear paper trail. The reversal didn't destabilize the design because the primary hierarchy (reviews above fold) was never in question.

Process — The Judgments, Not the Iterations

The Framing Judgment

Existing search performance data made the business case undeniable without needing new research. Choosing to lead with data — not user interviews, not competitive analysis — accelerated alignment across five stakeholders in the first meeting.

The Filter Judgment

Three approaches explored: zip code input, metro pill grid, free-form auto-suggest. Zip code required radius logic from engineering. Pills locked us into a pre-defined city list. Auto-suggest scaled to any city while guiding users toward results we had. One approach solved for UX, engineering complexity, and data coverage simultaneously.

The Scope Judgment

Write a Review CTAs and FAQ section were in the original brief. I removed both from scope early: Write a Review served a different user type and FAQs were high-effort for Phase 1. This sharpened the design's focus. Both were later confirmed as Phase 2 by the PM.

The Label Judgment

"Recently approved reviews" implied attorney approval, not platform moderation. Changed to "Recent reviews" after stakeholder feedback. Small change, significant trust implication.

If Not Me

Without the architectural reframing, this project would have shipped as a visual refresh. The directory structure would have stayed above the fold. The CTR gap — the actual business problem — would not have been addressed.

The filter system, the card redesign, and the scope decisions all follow from the one judgment that mattered most: this is a content hierarchy problem, not a visual one.

Lessons Learned

Documentation as a design tool

Maintaining a living decision log throughout the project meant that when the search bar decision was reversed in a final meeting, the team could trace the original rationale, understand the new constraint, and adjust without losing ground.

Stakeholder alignment is continuous

Two decisions that were "confirmed" got revisited. Having rationale on record made those conversations faster and less political. Alignment isn't a gate — it's a practice.
